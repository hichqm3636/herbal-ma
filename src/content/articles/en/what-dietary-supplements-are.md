People meet the phrase “dietary supplement” on packages, in shops, and in online discussion. The phrase is useful, but it is not a single worldwide legal stamp. This article explains the idea in educational terms: what the term usually points to, why official definitions differ, and how Herbal.ma uses it. It does not tell anyone what to take.

## A working description

In everyday language, a dietary supplement is a product sold to add something to what a person already eats and drinks. It is not presented as a meal in itself. Typical contents include vitamins, minerals, herbs or other plants, amino acids, and other substances that manufacturers place in a measured product.

That description is a starting point, not a diagnosis of any one bottle. The same shop shelf can hold a simple vitamin tablet, a powdered drink mix, and an herbal capsule. What they share is the claim that they *add to* dietary intake rather than *replace* ordinary food.

## Forms people commonly see

Official consumer materials list many physical forms. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) describes dietary supplements as products that are ingested — swallowed — and that may appear as pills, tablets, capsules, gummies, softgels, liquids, and powders. Some items look like a tea or a bar; under U.S. rules they still have to be labeled as a supplement and must not be represented as conventional food or as the sole item of a meal.

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements gives a similar picture for U.S. readers: tablets, capsules, gummies, powders, drinks, and energy bars are all among the forms people encounter. The point for this article is not to rank those forms. It is to show that “supplement” names a *role* (adding to the diet) more than a single shape.

## Legal definitions are local

Dietary supplements are regulated differently from medicines, and the wording of those rules changes from one jurisdiction to another. Herbal.ma does not treat any one country’s statute as a global fact.

**United States.** U.S. law defines a dietary supplement in the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994. In FDA’s summary, a dietary supplement is a product intended for ingestion that contains a “dietary ingredient” intended to supplement the diet. Dietary ingredients include vitamins and minerals; herbs and other botanicals; amino acids; dietary substances that are part of the food supply, such as enzymes and live microbials; and concentrates, metabolites, constituents, extracts, or combinations of those ingredients. The product must also be labeled as a dietary supplement or an equivalent phrase such as “herbal supplement.” FDA also distinguishes dietary ingredients from “other ingredients” such as fillers, binders, and flavorings.

The same U.S. materials stress that FDA’s rules for dietary supplements are not the same as its rules for prescription or over-the-counter medicines. That is a statement about U.S. oversight. It is not a claim that every country uses the same boundary, and it is not a claim that a product is automatically safe because it is sold as a supplement.

**European Union.** In the EU, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) describes food supplements as concentrated sources of nutrients or other substances with a nutritional or physiological effect, marketed in dose form such as pills, tablets, capsules, or liquids in measured doses. Under EU general food law, food supplements are treated as foods. Directive 2002/46/EC sets harmonized lists of vitamins, minerals, and their chemical sources that may be used, and it sets labeling requirements. Ingredients other than vitamins and minerals may follow other EU rules or national rules. Presentation and advertising must not attribute to food supplements the property of preventing, treating, or curing a human disease.

Those two systems already disagree in vocabulary (“dietary supplement” versus “food supplement”), in how botanicals are handled, and in what a label must say. Other countries use still other terms. Readers in Morocco, or anywhere else, should not assume that a U.S. Supplement Facts panel or an EU food-supplement label describes the product in their own market.

## Adding to the diet is not replacing food

Both the U.S. and EU educational texts return to the same practical idea: these products are meant to *supplement* a diet, not to stand in for varied food. EU labeling rules even require a statement that food supplements should not be used as a substitute for a varied diet. That requirement does not tell a reader which product to buy. It marks a limit on what the category is for.

Herbal.ma uses the same limit. An article about supplements is not a meal plan, and a nutrient name on a label is not proof that someone’s diet is incomplete.

## What Herbal.ma means by the term

When this site says “dietary supplement,” it means a product offered to add a vitamin, mineral, herb, or related substance to dietary intake, in a form people swallow or mix to swallow. The site discusses those products as educational subjects: what names mean, how labels are arranged in specific jurisdictions, and how evidence is graded.

The term does not mean:

- a medicine prescribed for a disease;
- a complete food;
- a ranking of brands;
- a personal recommendation.

If a later page quotes a U.S. or EU rule, it will say so. If a page cannot name the jurisdiction, it will stay with the general educational meaning above.

## What this article does not decide

This page does not decide whether any person needs a supplement. It does not decide whether a named ingredient prevents or treats a disease. It does not treat “natural” as a synonym for “safe.” It does not replace a conversation with a qualified health professional, and it does not rank products.

Those limits are part of the definition, not a footnote. Once the word “supplement” is stretched into a cure, a diet replacement, or a shopping instruction, it has left the meaning this article is willing to support.
