A supplement label is a document. It tells you how the maker has arranged names, amounts, and legal statements for a specific market. It does not, by itself, tell you what your body needs. This article teaches how to *read* that document. It does not certify any product and it does not convert a line on a label into a personal dose.

Label rules are local. The most detailed public example used here is the United States Supplement Facts panel, because the U.S. Food and Drug Administration publishes a chapter-length labeling guide. The European Union uses different required sentences. Other countries use other layouts. When a heading below refers to Supplement Facts, it refers to U.S. rules unless the text says otherwise.

## Start with identity, not with a health promise

Under U.S. rules, a dietary supplement must be labeled as a dietary supplement or with an equivalent phrase such as “herbal supplement.” That identity statement is easy to skip. It is the first clue that the package is being sold in the supplement category of that market, not as a conventional food and not as an approved medicine.

If a package in another country uses a different category name — for example “food supplement” in EU materials — the same caution applies: read the category the label actually claims, not the category you expected from a video or a foreign website.

## The nutrition panel is not a food label with a new title

In the United States the nutrition label for a dietary supplement is called a **Supplement Facts** panel, not a Nutrition Facts panel. FDA’s labeling guide says the panel must list the names and quantities of dietary ingredients present, the serving size, and the servings per container. Servings per container may be omitted when that number is already the same as the net quantity of contents (for example, 100 tablets with a serving of one tablet).

Those headings are mandatory *words* in U.S. labeling. A manufacturer may not replace “Serving Size” with a more casual phrase. That rigidity is useful for readers: the same phrases reappear from bottle to bottle in that market.

## Serving size is a labeled amount, not a personal instruction

FDA’s U.S. guide defines one serving of a dietary supplement as the maximum amount recommended on the label for consumption per eating occasion. If the directions say to take one to three tablets with breakfast, the serving size would be three tablets. If the label gives no recommendation, the serving size is one unit — a tablet, capsule, packet, or teaspoonful, for example.

Two reading traps follow.

First, serving size is set from the *label’s own directions*, not from an independent nutrition survey of how people eat. It is not a scientific finding about you.

Second, “amount per serving” is an amount *in that labeled serving*. If you mentally change the serving, the numbers on the panel no longer describe what you think you are comparing.

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements notes, for U.S. consumers, that the manufacturer suggests the serving size, and that a health care provider might consider a different amount appropriate. That sentence is a warning against treating the panel as a prescription. It is not permission for this article to recommend any amount.

## Amount per serving and % Daily Value

U.S. Supplement Facts panels declare quantitative amounts of dietary ingredients and, where a Daily Value exists, a **% Daily Value** (%DV). FDA’s consumer explainer describes Daily Values as reference amounts used to calculate %DV. The %DV is how much of a nutrient in a *single labeled serving* of that packaged product contributes toward that Daily Value. If a Daily Value is 300 micrograms and one labeled serving contains 30 micrograms, the %DV is 10%.

%DV is therefore a comparison tool on a U.S. label. It is not a personal medical target, and it is not a World Health Organization dose. Some dietary ingredients have no Daily Value; the panel then shows the amount by weight without a %DV.

EU food-supplement labels, as summarized by EFSA, do not copy this panel. They must include the names of the characterizing nutrients or substances, the portion recommended for daily consumption, a warning not to exceed that stated recommended daily dose, a statement that food supplements should not be used as a substitute for a varied diet, and a statement about keeping the product out of the reach of young children. Presentation must not attribute to the product the prevention, treatment, or cure of a human disease.

A reader comparing a U.S. bottle with an EU bottle is comparing two documents written under two rulebooks.

## Dietary ingredients and other ingredients

FDA distinguishes **dietary ingredients** — vitamins, minerals, herbs, amino acids, and related substances intended to supplement the diet — from **other ingredients** such as fillers, binders, preservatives, sweeteners, and flavorings. Both can appear on a package. Mixing them up is a common reading error: a long ingredient list is not automatically a long list of vitamins.

Where a U.S. product uses a proprietary blend, FDA’s labeling guide requires the total weight of the blend and the components in order of predominance by weight. That format tells you the *order* of amounts inside the blend. It does not, by itself, tell you the amount of each named component. This article records that limit. It does not interpret any blend as effective or ineffective.

## Administrative lines

Many packages also carry the name and place of business of the manufacturer, packer, or distributor, and a net quantity. Those lines answer “who is responsible for this package in this market?” They do not answer “does this ingredient work?” FDA’s questions-and-answers page lists those identity and quantity statements among the required U.S. label elements. It does not offer a method for judging a factory from its address.

## How to use this reading method

A practical order, for any market, is:

1. What category does the label claim (supplement, food supplement, or another local term)?
2. What is the labeled unit or portion, and is that unit defined by local rules or by the maker’s directions?
3. Which lines are amounts of characterizing ingredients, and which lines are other ingredients?
4. Which numbers are amounts by weight, and which — if any — are percent-of-reference figures such as a U.S. %DV?
5. Which sentences are warnings or legal limits (do not exceed a stated portion; not a substitute for a varied diet; not a disease treatment) rather than descriptions of contents?

If a line is unclear, the honest reading is “unclear,” not a guess that fills the gap with a health claim.

## What this article will not do

It will not recommend a dose. It will not recommend a brand. It will not certify that a product complies with any regulator. It will not treat a label claim as proof of efficacy. It will not tell a reader what amount they personally need.

Those refusals are the point of label literacy. A label is evidence of *how a product is presented in a given market*. Evidence about whether an ingredient does what advertising hopes it does belongs to a different kind of document, and to a different Herbal.ma article.
