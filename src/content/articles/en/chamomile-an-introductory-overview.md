“Chamomile” is a familiar word for a plant people meet in teas, in foods, and on some product labels. Familiarity is not the same as a single botanical identity. This article explains that problem of naming, and it describes how Herbal.ma treats chamomile as a knowledge subject. It does not discuss treatment of any condition.

Herbal.ma also keeps a separate Ingredient record for chamomile. This page is an introductory overview of the same subject.

## One common name, more than one plant

The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) states that **there are two types of chamomile: German chamomile and Roman chamomile**. Its fact sheet then says that it *focuses on* German chamomile. That sentence is the opposite of a collapse: the agency names two plants and then limits the sheet to one of them.

The same page lists Latin names used for the German plant: *Matricaria recutita*, *Chamomilla recutita*, and *Matricaria chamomilla*. A package, a menu, or a tea bag that says only “chamomile” has not yet told you which plant is in it, and it has not told you which Latin name the maker had in mind.

Herbal.ma therefore does not treat “German chamomile” as a universal alias for chamomile as a whole. The Ingredient record uses the common name as a heading for a naming cluster, not as a claim that one species is the entire subject.

If a later page needs to discuss one species in detail, it will have to name that species and source that narrower identity. This overview will not smuggle a species-level identity into every use of the English word.

## A name with a long documentary history

NCCIH notes that chamomile was described in ancient medical writings and that it was an important herb in ancient China, Egypt, Greece, and Rome. That is a historical statement about why the name is old and widely recognized.

History of mention is not a modern study. It does not identify the plant in a supermarket tea. It does not measure a preparation. It does not answer a health question about a person today. Cultural familiarity explains why readers look the name up. It is not evidence that a product on a current shelf does a named job.

## How people commonly encounter it

NCCIH describes chamomile as used orally in amounts commonly found in **teas and other foods**. That is the usage context this article is willing to record: an infusion or a food ingredient, not a therapy.

Tea and food context still leaves identity work to do. An infusion labeled chamomile may be one plant or the other, a mixture, or a flavored product that only borrows the name. The bag does not become a botanical certificate because it is sold next to other herbal teas.

This article does not describe essential oils, internal use of concentrated oils, or skin preparations. Those are different product types, and they are outside an identity overview of the plant name.

## Why the name appears on labels and in shops

Because the word is widely used for infusions and foods, it also appears on some products sold as dietary supplements in markets that have that category. A U.S. herbal product intended for oral use may be marketed as a dietary supplement; NCCIH notes that herbal products are regulated in different ways depending on composition, intended use, and how they are given. That is a U.S. regulatory observation. It is not a finding that a chamomile product in another country is a supplement, a food, or a medicine.

Seeing the name on a label answers “what word did the maker print?” It does not answer “which plant?” and it does not answer “does this do what advertising hopes?”

## How this page relates to the Ingredient record

The Ingredient record named chamomile is the stable entity for the common-name subject on Herbal.ma. This article does not replace it. It explains why a single record is still useful even though more than one plant shares the name: readers meet the name as a name, and the first educational task is to keep the naming problem visible.

Neither this article nor the Ingredient record is a treatment monograph, a safety sheet, or a shopping guide.

## What this article will not do

It will not claim that chamomile treats sleep problems, anxiety, infant colic, or inflammation. It will not discuss pregnancy or breastfeeding. It will not advise internal use of essential oil. It will not collapse German chamomile into the whole concept. It will not tell a reader to drink a tea for a health outcome.

If a later page on Herbal.ma takes up a specific plant species, a specific preparation, or a specific research question, it will need its own sources and its own risk classification. This overview stops at identity, naming, and ordinary food-and-infusion context.
