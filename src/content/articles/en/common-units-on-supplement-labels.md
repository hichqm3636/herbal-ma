A number on a supplement label is not a free-standing medical fact. It is an amount written in a chosen unit, for a labeled portion, under a local rule. This article explains the units readers meet most often. It does not convert those units into a personal dose, and it does not treat a U.S. panel as the world’s default.

The abbreviations below are the ones the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) lists on its consumer page about Daily Values: grams (g), milligrams (mg), micrograms (mcg), and international units (IU), among others. Other markets may print the same metric abbreviations, a different reference figure, or both.

## Weight units: mg and mcg

A **milligram** (mg) is one thousandth of a gram. A **microgram** (mcg or µg) is one thousandth of a milligram, or one millionth of a gram. Those relationships are ordinary metric arithmetic. They do not tell you how much of a nutrient a person should take.

On a U.S. Supplement Facts panel, the quantitative amount of a vitamin or mineral is supposed to use the same unit that FDA uses for that nutrient’s Daily Value. FDA’s labeling guide gives a simple illustration: vitamin C is listed in milligrams because its Daily Value is stated in milligrams. The current U.S. Daily Value table uses milligrams for many minerals and some vitamins, and micrograms for others. The unit on the line is therefore a labeling choice tied to that table, not a hint about potency.

English-language U.S. labels commonly print **mcg** rather than the SI symbol **µg**. Both name the same unit. If a package from another country uses µg, µ, or a local abbreviation, the honest reading is still “microgram,” unless the label defines a different unit.

Two practical traps follow from the arithmetic.

First, 1,000 micrograms equal 1 milligram. Mixing the two abbreviations without converting them makes two products look similar when they are not, or different when they are the same mass.

Second, the number is the amount *in the labeled serving*, not a daily total and not an amount per kilogram of body weight. Changing the serving in your head changes the meaning of the number.

## International units are not a mass

An **international unit** (IU) is a measure of biological activity that a standards body has assigned to a particular substance. It is not a gram. Because the assignment is substance-specific, there is no single “IU to milligram” factor that works for every vitamin.

NIH’s Office of Dietary Supplements states the vitamin D factor plainly: **1 microgram of vitamin D equals 40 IU**. That sentence is about vitamin D. It is not a conversion table for vitamin A, vitamin E, or any other ingredient. If a label uses IU for a different substance, this article will not invent a factor for it.

U.S. consumer materials now list the Daily Value for vitamin D in micrograms (20 mcg on FDA’s current reference list). Some packages still show IU as well. Seeing both units on one line is a unit-literacy problem, not a dosing instruction. If you need to compare a vitamin D figure written in IU with one written in mcg, the ODS factor above is the vitamin D factor. If the substance is not vitamin D, do not reuse that factor.

## Percent Daily Value is a U.S. reference figure

**Percent Daily Value** (%DV) is a U.S. label convention. FDA describes Daily Values as reference amounts used to calculate %DV. The %DV is how much of a nutrient in *one labeled serving* of that packaged product contributes toward that Daily Value.

FDA’s own example: if a Daily Value is 300 micrograms and one labeled serving contains 30 micrograms, the %DV is 10%. The arithmetic is:

> (amount in the labeled serving ÷ Daily Value) × 100

FDA’s labeling guide uses the same structure: divide the quantitative amount by the established Daily Value and multiply by 100. The guide also notes that some dietary ingredients have no Daily Value; those lines show an amount by weight and a footnote such as “Daily Value not established.”

What %DV is not:

- a World Health Organization dose;
- a personal blood-test target;
- a ranking of brands;
- a statement that you “need” the remaining percentage from a bottle.

FDA’s consumer page also offers a coarse U.S. food-label guide that 5% DV or less per serving is considered low and 20% DV or more is considered high. That guide is about reading a U.S. packaged-food or supplement line. It is not a prescription, and it is not a global nutrition standard.

Daily Values themselves are U.S. figures. They can change when FDA updates the table. An older bottle and a newer bottle may use different Daily Values for the same nutrient name. Comparing %DV across years or across countries without checking which table was used is a reading error.

## Other jurisdictions do not copy this panel

In the European Union, food-supplement labels, as summarized by the European Food Safety Authority, must name the characterizing nutrients or substances and state the portion recommended for daily consumption. They must also carry a warning not to exceed that stated portion and a statement that food supplements should not replace a varied diet. That is a different document from a U.S. %DV column.

A reader in Morocco, or anywhere else, should not assume that mg, mcg, IU, or %DV on a foreign website describe the product on a local shelf. Metric abbreviations are widely used. The *reference* behind a percentage, and the legal meaning of a “recommended portion,” are local.

## How to read a unit without turning it into advice

A durable order of questions is:

1. Which substance is named on this line?
2. Which unit is printed (mg, mcg/µg, IU, or a percentage)?
3. Is that unit a mass, an activity unit, or a percent of a local reference?
4. Is the amount given per labeled serving, per unit (tablet, capsule), or per a portion defined by another rulebook?
5. If two labels use different units for the same name, is a substance-specific conversion actually known — or is the comparison incomplete?

If the conversion is unknown, the accurate sentence is “these two numbers are not yet in the same unit,” not a guess.

## What this article will not do

It will not tell you how many milligrams you should take. It will not treat 100% DV as a personal requirement. It will not publish a therapeutic-equivalence table. It will not turn the vitamin D IU factor into a dose. It will not certify that a number on a package is analytically correct.

Units make labels comparable *when the same substance, the same unit, and the same rulebook are in play*. Once any of those three changes, the comparison has to be rebuilt. That limit is the whole of unit literacy.
