“Vitamin D” is a nutrient name that appears on food packages, on fortified products, and on supplement labels. This article identifies what that name refers to. It is not a protocol for sun exposure, a dose table, or a discussion of any disease.

Herbal.ma also keeps a separate Ingredient record for vitamin D. This page is an introductory overview of the same subject.

## What the name refers to

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements describes vitamin D — also called calciferol — as a **fat-soluble vitamin**. It is naturally present in a few foods, added to others, and available as a dietary supplement. The body can also produce vitamin D when ultraviolet light from sunlight reaches the skin.

Those four settings (unfortified food, fortified food, supplements, and skin synthesis) are why the same name shows up in very different places. They are not four competing brands, and they are not a ranking of which source a person should choose.

Fat-soluble, in this identification, means that vitamin D dissolves in fat rather than in water. The fact sheet uses that chemical class to place vitamin D among other fat-soluble vitamins. It is not, here, a direction to take any product with a meal.

## More than one chemical form

In foods and dietary supplements, vitamin D has two main forms, which the NIH fact sheet names as **D2 (ergocalciferol)** and **D3 (cholecalciferol)**. The two forms differ chemically in their side chains. Both appear in the food supply and in supplement products.

This matters for reading. “Vitamin D” on a label is a family name, not a promise that a single molecule is present. A product may contain one form, the other, or a mixture. Treating D3 as if it *were* vitamin D, or treating D2 as a mere alias, collapses a distinction the fact sheet is careful to keep.

This article does not compare the two forms for “strength.” It records that they exist, that they are not identical, and that neither form is the whole concept.

The NIH fact sheet also notes that vitamin D obtained from sun, food, or supplements is not yet the activated form used in the body’s tissues; activation involves further chemical steps. That is a biochemical identity point. It is not a reason, in this article, to recommend a blood test or a product.

## Where the name appears in food and on packages

Very few foods contain vitamin D unless it is added. The NIH fact sheet lists natural occurrences in the flesh of fatty fish, in fish liver oils, and in smaller amounts in beef liver, egg yolks, and cheese, with some mushrooms contributing as well. **Fortified foods** supply much of the vitamin D in U.S. diets: the fact sheet gives U.S. milk, many plant-milk alternatives, and some breakfast cereals, juices, yogurts, and margarines as examples of products to which vitamin D is added.

Fortification rules are local. A U.S. example does not describe every country’s milk, margarine, or cereal. If a Moroccan or other local product is fortified, that fact belongs on *that* product’s label, not as a global assumption.

Dietary supplements are another place the name appears: as a single-nutrient product, in combination products, or inside a multivitamin. The presence of the name identifies an ingredient line. It does not identify a brand, a needed amount, or a health outcome.

## Why the name appears on labels

Readers meet the name on food packages and on supplement labels because vitamin D occurs in some foods, is added to others, and is sold as a dietary supplement. In the United States, packaged foods and dietary supplements may declare vitamin D using that market’s labeling conventions. This article records that the name appears. It does not teach how to read a percent Daily Value, convert international units, or calculate a labeled amount.
